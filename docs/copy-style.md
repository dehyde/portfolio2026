# Case-study copy style

## Purpose

Write a clear, factual explanation of a complex product-design problem. The reader should understand the organizations, products, people, and constraints without needing to decode a metaphor or a design-process narrative.

## Core rules

1. Lead with concrete entities: named teams, products, personas, governance levels, and numbers where they clarify scope.
2. State relationships as facts. Say who owns a product, who it serves, and where a responsibility sits instead of narrating a concept "meeting" another concept.
3. Use one dense, well-structured sentence for a section's main proposition. It should orient the reader before the supporting detail begins.
4. Use exact project terminology: account-level and project-level governance, data definitions, custom-data experience, Autodesk Construction Cloud, AEC Data, and the relevant persona names.
5. State friction directly. Describe the conflicting requirements or constraints without rhetoric, drama, or implied mystery.
6. Avoid vague abstractions and stock portfolio language, including "a system meets a product," "the tension," "unlocking," "seamless," and generic references to consistency, alignment, or flexibility without saying whose and where.
7. Keep first-person contribution out of Context. Context describes the situation; individual role and collaboration belong in the introduction, Background, or a dedicated role section.
8. Make the reading sequence cumulative. Each proposition may rely only on information already introduced; define unfamiliar platform or domain concepts before using them as shorthand.
9. Match tense to time: use present tense for ongoing products, team ownership, and current platform structure; use past tense for the pre-project problem, decisions made, and work completed.
10. Keep claims precise and supportable. Do not imply outcomes, research findings, ownership, or organizational agreement that the evidence does not establish.

## Content structure

Each section and subsection follows the same reading order:

1. Semantic title: what this part is about.
2. Proposition: the primary factual point, stated in one sentence.
3. Evidence: details, artifacts, process, or examples that substantiate it.

## Pre-project problem example

Account managers needed to enforce data definition consistency across projects, while construction project personas needed more flexibility in defining their own standards.

This uses past tense because it describes the situation before the project began.
